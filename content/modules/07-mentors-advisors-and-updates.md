## What this module is for

By the end of this module you'll know how to find people who can help you, how to keep them helping, when it makes sense to turn a mentor into a formal advisor, and how to write a short monthly update email. You'll finish with your first update ready to send.

Many first-time founders start without a network of people who have built a company before. That's normal, and it's fixable. Plenty of experienced people are willing to give a stranger fifteen minutes if the request is clear and the founder is obviously doing the work. What they won't do is take on a vague, open-ended role for someone they've never met.

The monthly update ties this module together. It turns one-off conversations into ongoing relationships, because the people who helped you get to see what happened next. Before you read on, write down the names of three people who have already helped you, however small the help. They'll be the first readers of your update.

## Find mentors by asking for something small

A mentor is someone with relevant experience who helps you informally, usually for free, because they enjoy it or because someone once helped them. You'll rarely find one by asking "Will you be my mentor?" That question asks a stranger to commit an unknown amount of time to an unknown purpose. Ask instead for one specific, small piece of help. Mentorship tends to grow out of a few good exchanges, not out of a title.

Start a list of fifteen to twenty people. The best candidates are often not famous. Look for:

- people a few years ahead of you who recently solved the problem you're stuck on
- people who used to work in your customers' industry
- founders whose companies sell to the same customers but don't compete with you
- former managers, teachers and colleagues, who already know how you work
- people who answer questions thoughtfully in online communities in your field

You can find them through your own contacts, alumni groups, local founder meetups, industry associations, and the free advice services that many governments and universities run for small businesses. Next to each name, write the one question that person is better placed to answer than anyone else on your list. If you can't think of one, they're probably not the right person yet.

Then write a short message. Say who you are in one line, why you chose them, what you're asking for and how long it will take, and give them an easy way to say no.

> Hi Maria, I'm building a booking tool for community sports clubs, and I saw that you ran registrations for a large youth league for six years. I'm stuck on one problem: how clubs chase parents who haven't paid. Could I ask you three questions about it in a 15-minute call next week? If email suits you better, I can send them instead, and if now isn't a good time, that's completely fine.

Send five messages this week. Expect some silence, since people are busy; one polite follow-up after a week is fine. When someone says yes, prepare: send a little context in advance, ask your questions, finish on time and write down what you heard.

## What makes a mentor want to keep helping

People keep helping founders who make their help feel worthwhile. Three habits do most of the work.

**Show progress.** Mentors are encouraged when the thing they helped with moves forward. It doesn't have to be a big win. "We spoke to twelve more clubs and four want to try it" is progress.

**Follow through, then close the loop.** If someone suggests something, try it, and tell them what happened, even if it didn't work. A two-line message a few weeks later is often the moment a one-off call becomes an ongoing relationship.

> Hi Maria, thanks again for suggesting we send payment reminders from each club's own email address rather than ours. We tried it with three clubs, and unpaid registrations fell from about a third to under a fifth. I'll share the full numbers in our next monthly update.

**Make specific asks.** "Any advice?" is hard to answer. "Do you know anyone who runs a league with more than twenty teams?" is easy. When you ask for an introduction, include a short paragraph about your company that your mentor can forward without rewriting, and leave the decision to introduce you up to them.

A few other habits help. Respect your mentors' time and judgment, but remember that you make the decisions. Mentors often disagree with each other, and that's useful information, not a problem. If you decide not to follow someone's advice, tell them why. Say thank you, and look for ways to help in return, such as sharing what you've learned or introducing them to someone useful.

Be cautious with anyone who asks for payment or equity before they've helped at all. Paid coaching can be valuable, but it's a purchase, so treat it like one: know exactly what you're buying and for how long.

## Advisors versus mentors

A mentor relationship is informal: no contract, no fixed commitment, and you can have several at once. An **advisor** has a defined role. You agree what they'll help with, roughly how much time they'll give and what they get in return, and you write it down. Some founders list advisors in investor materials or on their website, which you should only do with the advisor's permission.

An advisor role can make sense when you need ongoing help in a specific area, the person has already shown they'll deliver, and you can describe what success would look like. Imagine a founder building software for small pharmacies who needs regular guidance on pharmacy regulations from someone who used to work at a regulator. That's a good reason. Offering the role to a well-known person mainly because their name might impress investors is not.

Here's how advisor arrangements commonly work. This is a general description, not legal or tax advice, and practice differs between countries.

- **Compensation** is usually a small equity grant rather than cash. At early-stage startups it's commonly a fraction of one percent of the company, often somewhere between about 0.1% and 1%, depending on how early the company is and how involved the advisor will be. That's an observation of common practice, not a recommendation for your company.
- **Vesting** means the advisor earns the equity over time instead of receiving it all at once. Advisor grants often vest monthly over one or two years, sometimes with a short **cliff**, such as three months, before which nothing vests. If the relationship doesn't work out early, little or nothing has been given away.
- **The form of the equity** depends on where and how your company is set up. It might be share options, shares or a virtual share arrangement, and each can have different tax effects for the advisor.
- **A written agreement** should cover what the advisor will do, how the equity vests, confidentiality, who owns any work they produce and how either side can end the arrangement.

Standard advisor agreement templates exist, but tax and securities rules vary by country, so have a lawyer review the agreement before anyone signs. For general background, see [Wikipedia's article on vesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesting). If you think you need an advisor, write down the area, the time you'd want each month and how you'd know it's working before you approach anyone.

## The monthly update email

A monthly update is a short email you send on the same day each month to people who care how your company is doing: mentors, advisors, early users who've asked to hear from you, friends who've helped, and current or possible future investors who've agreed to receive it. Once you're used to it, it takes about an hour to write.

A good update uses the same structure every month:

1. **A one-line summary**, so a busy reader gets the point immediately.
2. **Key metrics**: the same three to five numbers each month, with last month's figure beside each, such as paying customers, monthly revenue, active users and months of cash left.
3. **Wins**: what went well, in a few short bullets.
4. **Lowlights**: what went badly and what you're doing about it.
5. **What you learned**: one insight about your customers, market or product.
6. **Asks**: one to three specific requests a reader could act on today.
7. **Thank-yous**: the people who helped, named if they're happy to be.

Here's a lowlight written well:

> Two of our eight paying clubs cancelled this month. Both said their volunteer treasurer had left and nobody else knew how to use the tool. We're now adding a second admin to every account by default and writing a five-minute handover guide. Next month we'll report whether cancellations drop.

Sending updates consistently builds trust in a way no single meeting can. A meeting shows where you are today; a year of updates shows which direction you're heading and whether you do what you say you'll do. Each audience gets something different:

- **Mentors** see their advice turn into results, which makes them more likely to keep helping and to introduce you to others.
- **Early users** see that you listen and that the product is improving. Consider sending them a shorter version focused on product changes and requests for feedback, without financial details.
- **Future investors** can watch your progress for months before you ever ask them for money, so if you do ask, they already know how you work.

Honesty is what makes this work. Keep the same metrics in a bad month; readers notice when a number quietly disappears. Keep the email short enough to read in about two minutes. Ask before adding anyone to your list, make it easy to unsubscribe, and leave out anything confidential about customers. Assume any update could be forwarded.

## Do the work

Open the monthly update builder and write this month's update.

1. Enter your company name and the month.
2. Choose three to five metrics you can measure every month, and write each with last month's figure. If you haven't launched, use honest early numbers, such as customer conversations held, waitlist sign-ups or prototype tests completed.
3. List your wins, then write your lowlights with what you're doing about each one.
4. Write one thing you learned.
5. Write one to three asks. Each should say exactly what you need, who could help and by when.
6. Add your thank-yous, then write the one-line summary last, once you know what the month was really about.

You're done when you have an email someone could read in two minutes, with at least one ask a reader could act on today, that you'd be comfortable seeing forwarded. Send it to at least five people, including the three you named at the start, and put a reminder in your calendar for the same day next month.
