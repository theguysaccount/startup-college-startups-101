## What the job actually is

The goal of this module is to show you what a founder running a young company actually spends time on, and to help you turn that into a one-page operating plan for your next 90 days. You don't need a team or funding to use it. If you're working on your idea alone, the job is still yours.

Whatever your title, the work in the first year comes down to five jobs that nobody else will do for you:

- **Set direction.** Decide which customer you serve, which problem you solve for them and what to build next. Just as important, decide what you won't build.
- **Find customers.** Talk to them, sell to them and learn from them. In the first year, the founder is usually the entire sales team.
- **Recruit.** Persuade people to help: a co-founder, a first hire, a freelancer, an adviser, an early customer who gives you their time. Even with no budget, you are always recruiting.
- **Raise money, only if you need it.** Fundraising can take months of your attention, and many good companies never raise outside money at all. They grow from revenue, savings, grants or part-time income instead. Raise when money is what stands between you and your next milestone, not because it seems expected.
- **Keep the team executing.** Make sure everyone, even a team of two, knows the priorities, has what they need and finishes what they start.

Almost everything else can wait, or deserves far less time than founders give it: the logo, the office, a polished website before anyone has paid you, the ideal legal structure for a company with no customers yet. For any task, ask: if I stopped doing this for a month, would a customer notice? If not, it probably isn't one of the five jobs.

## Deciding without all the facts

You'll make dozens of decisions a week with less information than you'd like. Waiting for certainty feels responsible, but waiting is a decision too, and it has a cost: every week you wait is a week you aren't learning.

To sort a decision quickly, ask two questions. If this turns out to be wrong, how much will it cost? And how long would it take to undo? Most decisions then fall into one of two groups:

- **Cheap to undo.** Which invoicing tool to use, what to call a feature, which street to hand out flyers on. Decide quickly, often the same day and often alone. If you're wrong, you'll notice and switch.
- **Expensive to undo.** Splitting ownership with a co-founder, quitting your job, signing a long contract, hiring someone full-time, taking investment. Slow down. Write out the options, sleep on it and talk to someone who has made the same decision. Where ownership, contracts or investment are involved, have a lawyer review the documents before you sign.

For the expensive ones, write a short decision note: what you decided, what you rejected, what you expect to happen and when you'll check. Months later, compare what you expected with what actually happened. It's one of the few ways to improve your judgment on purpose.

> A founder building a tool that lets small cafés reorder milk, bread and coffee in one place writes: "Decision: launch with one supplier's products only. Rejected: every supplier in the city at once. Expect: cafés will accept a single supplier if it saves them time, and we'll learn faster with one connection to maintain. Check: after 10 cafés have used it for a month."

Some decisions don't need making yet. Expanding to a second city, designing a premium plan, hiring a marketer: if nothing breaks by waiting, put the decision on hold and write down what would make you decide, such as "hire a developer once 10 cafés have paid for two months in a row". A deliberate "not yet" is very different from avoiding a decision.

## Protecting your focus

Founders rarely fail from laziness. More often they spread themselves across too many reasonable-looking things: a second type of customer, a conference, a competition, a feature for one demanding prospect, a partnership that "could be big". Each makes sense on its own. Together they leave no time for the work that shows whether the idea works at all. Most startups fail, and scattered effort makes it more likely that you run out of money or energy before you learn whether your idea works.

Focus starts with choosing. Pick **three priorities for the next 90 days** and write each as a result you can check, with a number and a date: "15 cafés ordering through the tool every week by 30 June", not "work on sales". Three is enough to make progress and few enough to remember.

Next, write down **what you will stop doing**: things you'll drop or pause because they don't serve those three priorities. Common candidates:

- networking events and coffee meetings with no clear purpose
- features for people who haven't committed to using or paying
- redesigning the website or logo again
- reading startup advice when you could be talking to customers, this course included

Finally, protect your time in practice:

- Block at least two long stretches a week, three hours or more, for building, writing or thinking, and treat them as seriously as a customer meeting.
- Put meetings on the same days so the other days stay unbroken.
- Check email and messages at set times instead of all day.
- Before you say yes to anything new, ask which of your three priorities it moves forward. If the answer is none, say "not now".

Every yes uses time that could have gone somewhere else, which economists call [opportunity cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opportunity_cost). Saying no quickly and politely gets easier with practice.

## A weekly operating rhythm

Plans fail quietly when nobody looks at them. A weekly rhythm is a few repeating habits that keep your plan in front of you, and it should take a couple of hours a week in total. Here is a simple version for a solo founder or a small team:

1. **Monday, 30 minutes: plan.** Look at your key metric and last week's commitments. Choose the three most important tasks for the week, each tied to one of your 90-day priorities. If you have a co-founder or team, agree on them together.
2. **Daily, 10 minutes: check in.** Share what got done, what's next and what's stuck. If you work alone, write those three lines for yourself.
3. **Two or three blocks a week: customer time.** Calls, visits, demos and support. Put them in your calendar first, before anything else fills the space.
4. **Friday, 30 minutes: review.** What moved the metric, what didn't happen and what did customers teach you? Write five or six lines and send them to a co-founder or adviser, or keep them in a file. You don't need investors to write a weekly update, and having one makes asking for help much easier.
5. **Once a month: check direction.** Are the three priorities still right? Adjust if you've learned something important, and write a fresh plan every 90 days.

The rhythm only works if you watch the right number. Choose **one key metric** that shows customers getting real value and that can go down as well as up. Before you have a product, it might be customer conversations held each week or people who agree to a paid trial. After launch, it might be weekly active customers, repeat orders or revenue. Avoid numbers that only ever grow, or that feel good without telling you anything, such as total sign-ups since launch or social media followers.

> The café ordering founder checks one number every Monday: cafés that placed at least one order through the tool in the past seven days. In April, sign-ups doubled after a local news story, but that number barely moved. It told him the real problem was getting new cafés to their first order, so he spent May sitting with new café owners while they set up.

## When to stop doing it yourself

At the start, doing things yourself is usually right. Answering support messages, setting up customers by hand and closing the first sales show you what customers struggle with and what good work looks like. You can't hand off a job well until you understand it.

But a founder who keeps doing everything becomes the reason things are slow. Signs it's time to hand something off:

- It repeats every week, and you could write the steps on one page.
- It keeps pushing your three priorities aside.
- Someone else could do it nearly as well, and nearly as well is good enough.
- Other people are waiting on you before they can move.
- It's specialist work where mistakes are expensive, such as bookkeeping, tax or contracts. Use a qualified professional early, because the rules differ from country to country.

Handing off doesn't always mean hiring. Roughly in order of cost, you can drop the task, automate it, use a template, trade skills with another founder, pay a freelancer or hire. Before handing anything off, ask whether it needs doing at all.

When you do hand off a task, agree on what "done" looks like, write down how you do it, do it together once, then watch the other person do it. After that, check the results weekly rather than every step.

A few things stay with you for a long time: talking to customers, setting direction, the final say on who joins and, if you raise, the fundraising itself. You can get advice on all of these, but you can't outsource understanding your customer.

Getting help also means asking for it, and you don't need a famous network to start. Former colleagues, people in your customers' industry, local founder meetups, online founder communities, alumni groups and the government small-business services many countries offer are all good places to ask. Keep each request small and specific: "Could you read this one-page plan and tell me what's missing?" gets far more replies than "Could I get your advice sometime?"

## Do the work

Fill in the founder operating plan builder at the end of this module, from top to bottom:

1. **Plan period.** Exact start and end dates, 90 days apart.
2. **Top three priorities.** Results with a number and a date, not activities. If you have more than three, choose; the rest can wait for the next plan.
3. **What you'll stop doing.** At least two things, and at least one should be hard to give up. If the list is painless, you haven't really chosen.
4. **Weekly rhythm.** One line per habit, with a day and time, so you can copy each straight into your calendar.
5. **Key metric.** One number, where it stands today (zero is fine) and where you want it in 90 days.
6. **Decisions on hold.** Each with the reason it can wait and what would make you decide.
7. **Who you'll ask for help.** A person or group, and a specific request.

You're done when the plan fits on one page, someone who has never heard of your company could read it in two minutes and tell you what you're focused on and what you're not, and your Monday planning and Friday review are already in your calendar. Then send the plan to one person you trust and ask them to check in with you in a month.
