## Do you need a co-founder?

This module helps you decide whether you need a co-founder, find people worth working with, test the fit on real work, pitch your idea honestly and settle the hard questions before anyone commits. By the end you will have a short pitch you can send to a potential co-founder and a checklist of what you will put in writing.

You don't have to have a co-founder. Many companies are started and run by one person. A co-founder makes sense when the work at the heart of the business is work you can't do well yourself, or when the load is more than one person can carry for years. Three questions help you decide:

1. What two or three things must this company do well in its first year? For a booking app for physiotherapy clinics, that might be building a reliable product and selling to clinic owners.
2. Which of those can you already do at a decent level?
3. For the rest, would a freelancer, an adviser or a later hire be enough, or does it need someone who owns the outcome with you?

If you decide to look, look for skills that differ from yours and values that match. Two founders who both love building and avoid selling will build a lot and sell very little. Differences in skill help. Differences in values, ambition, appetite for risk and working pace cause far more trouble. A friend you enjoy is not automatically a good co-founder, and an impressive work history isn't enough if you disagree about how big and how fast the company should grow.

Not everyone on your first team needs to be a co-founder. A freelancer can build a prototype, an adviser can introduce you to customers, and an early employee can join for a salary and a smaller stake. A co-founder shares responsibility for the whole company, usually owns a large part of it and is hard to replace. Keep that role for people you would trust to run the company for a month without you.

## Meet people, then work together before you commit

Many co-founders knew each other before they started, through work, study or a shared project. If you don't have a startup network, build relationships by doing things with people rather than relying on networking events. Good places to look:

- People you have already worked or studied with.
- Communities where your customers, or the skills you need, gather: industry forums, professional associations, open-source projects, design and developer groups.
- Hackathons and local meetups, where you can see how someone works under time pressure.
- Online co-founder matching platforms, some free and run by accelerators or universities.
- Your customer interviews. Someone who knows the industry from the inside may be a better partner than a stranger with the right job title.

When you meet someone promising, don't ask them to be your co-founder in the first conversation. Suggest a small project instead: real work, two to six weeks long, with a clear finish. You might run ten customer interviews together and write up what you learned, build a clickable prototype and test it with five users, or get three businesses to agree to a trial.

While you work, pay attention to how it goes, and invite them to do the same with you. Do you each deliver what you promised, when you promised it? How do you handle disagreement? Does either of you hide bad news? Can you both work without being chased? A few weeks of real work will tell you more than months of coffee meetings.

> A founder building a scheduling tool for home-care agencies met a former agency coordinator at a local health-tech meetup. Instead of discussing shares, they agreed to spend four weekends visiting agencies together. They interviewed twelve coordinators, disagreed twice about which feature to build first, and settled both arguments by going back to their interview notes. How they handled those disagreements told them more than the interviews did.

## Pitch the idea honestly

Asking someone to join means asking them to give up time, income and other opportunities. Give them a pitch as honest as the one you would want to hear. People who join because of hype tend to leave when the hype meets reality. Cover three things:

1. **What you've learned.** Your evidence so far: who you've spoken to, what they said and what they did, such as signing up, paying or asking for a trial. Keep facts separate from interpretation.
2. **What you need.** The role you want filled, why it matters and the time it takes: ten hours a week for three months, or full-time from a set date.
3. **What's uncertain.** The assumptions you haven't proven, how much money there is (often none), how long you can go without income, and the real chance that it doesn't work. Most startups fail; say so.

> Here's where I am. I've spoken to fifteen clinic managers, and eleven described the same scheduling problem without being prompted. Two asked to try something as soon as it exists. What I don't know yet is whether they'll pay, or whether their head offices will let them switch tools. I need someone who can build the first version and own the product. It's unpaid for now, and I can cover our costs for about six months. Before either of us decides anything, I'd like us to do a small project together for a month.

Then ask for their honest reaction, including the reasons they think it might fail. Give them time to think, and treat a no as useful information rather than an insult.

## The conversations to have before you start

Many co-founder disputes grow from things both people assumed but never said. Before you commit, talk through each of these topics and write down what you agree.

### Roles

Who owns which areas: product, engineering, sales, operations, finance, hiring? Owning an area means making its day-to-day decisions without asking permission, which matters more than titles. Agree how you'll revisit roles as the company grows.

### Decision-making

Which decisions need both of you, and which can one person make alone? How will you break a deadlock: final say for whoever owns that area, an adviser you both trust, or another rule you agree on now? Hold a regular check-in (weekly suits many teams) so small problems surface before they grow.

### Time commitment

Full-time or part-time, and starting when? If one of you is keeping a job for now, agree for how long and what will trigger the switch to full-time, such as a fixed date or the first five paying customers. Uneven effort that nobody discussed is a common source of resentment.

### Money

How much can each of you put in, and is it a loan, a gift to the company or an investment in exchange for shares? How long can each of you go without a salary, given your rent, dependents and debts? Many good companies never raise outside money, so plan to live on savings, part-time income or early revenue rather than counting on investors.

## Equity, vesting and what happens if someone leaves

This section explains how these arrangements commonly work. It is not legal or tax advice, and the rules differ from country to country. Have a lawyer draft or review your actual agreement.

### Splitting equity

Equity means ownership of the company, usually held as shares. There is no correct formula for splitting it. Some teams split equally; others weight the split by role, time commitment, money contributed or expected future contribution. Many founders give more weight to the years of work ahead than to who had the idea. Whatever you choose, each of you should be able to explain why the split is fair, and it should still feel fair in two years if the company is doing well.

### How vesting works

[Vesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesting) means founders earn their shares over time instead of owning them outright from day one. A common pattern, especially in US startups, is four years with a one-year cliff:

- Nothing vests during the first 12 months.
- At 12 months (the cliff), a quarter of the shares vest at once.
- The rest vest in equal monthly amounts over the following three years.

Often founders receive all their shares at the start, but the company has the right to buy back any unvested shares, usually at the low price the founder paid, if that founder leaves. Some agreements add acceleration, where vesting speeds up if the company is sold.

> Two founders each hold 50% of a meal-planning app for shift workers, on a four-year schedule with a one-year cliff. One leaves after 18 months. Since 18 of the 48 months have passed, 37.5% of their shares have vested, so they keep about 19% of the company and the company can buy back the rest. Had they left at month ten, before the cliff, they would typically keep nothing.

Vesting protects whoever stays. Without it, a co-founder who leaves after three months could keep a large stake in a company that others spend years building.

Tax treatment varies widely. In the US, for example, founders whose shares are subject to vesting commonly file what's called an 83(b) election with the IRS, the US tax authority, within 30 days of receiving the shares; missing that deadline can mean a much larger tax bill later. Other countries have their own rules, so speak to a local accountant or lawyer before any shares are issued.

### If someone leaves

Decide this while you still like each other:

- What happens to their vested and unvested shares.
- Whether they keep any role, title or board seat.
- That everything each founder creates for the company, such as code, designs and customer lists, belongs to the company. A signed assignment of intellectual property to the company is standard.
- What happens if a founder stops doing the work but doesn't leave.

In the UK and parts of Europe, agreements often treat "good leavers" (for example, someone leaving because of illness) differently from "bad leavers" (for example, someone leaving to join a competitor).

Put all of this in a written founders' agreement (in some countries it forms part of a shareholders' agreement) and have a lawyer review it before shares are issued. If cost is a barrier, look for university law clinics, startup support programs or legal aid services for new businesses.

## Do the work

Use the co-founder pitch builder below. It produces a one-page pitch you can send to a potential co-founder, together with the checklist of topics you'll agree on in writing.

- Write the problem in one line, the way a customer would say it.
- Under what you've learned, list evidence rather than opinions: how many conversations, what people did, what surprised you.
- Describe the role by what the person would own and achieve, not just a title.
- Be specific about time: hours per week and start dates, for both of you.
- List at least five topics for the written agreement, drawn from the last two sections.
- Choose a first project with a clear finish, and set the decision date shortly after it ends.

You're done when someone you respect could read the pitch and understand exactly what you're asking, what you know and what you don't. Then send it, do the project together and decide on the date you set. Whatever you decide, don't issue shares until your agreement is written down and reviewed by a lawyer.
