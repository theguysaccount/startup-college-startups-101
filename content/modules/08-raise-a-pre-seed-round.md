## Decide whether to raise at all

This module helps you decide whether to take outside investment now and, if you do, plan a small first round carefully. Nothing here is legal or investment advice. Securities rules, the laws about selling shares and similar investments, differ by country, and any document you sign should be reviewed by a lawyer who practices where your company is registered.

Start with the most important fact: most companies never raise money from investors, and many good businesses never need to. Most startups fail, whether or not they raise. Raising isn't proof that a company is working; customers paying you is a much better sign.

Investment also changes what's expected of you. Many early-stage investors expect most of their bets to fail and rely on a few huge successes, so they want companies that could grow very large, quickly. A steady, profitable business is a perfectly good goal, but it may not suit them.

Look at the alternatives first:

- **Customers' money.** Pre-orders, deposits, paid pilots and annual plans paid upfront fund the business and prove demand at the same time.
- **Revenue and bootstrapping.** Growing from what you earn, perhaps alongside part-time work, is slower, but you keep ownership and control.
- **Grants and competitions.** Many governments and foundations fund new companies without taking ownership, such as the US [SBIR program](https://www.sbir.gov), Innovate UK and the European Innovation Council. Look for your own country's innovation agency.

Be careful with personal debt, and only take money from friends and family who can afford to lose all of it and understand that they might.

Raising may make sense if you need money to reach a milestone you can't reach any other way, the company could plausibly grow very large, and you're willing to share ownership and report to investors for years. It also takes months of founder time. Before you read on, write one sentence: what would the money let you do that you couldn't do without it?

## Size the round to a milestone

Founders and investors often describe a round in terms of runway: how many months the money lets you operate before it runs out. The useful question isn't "How much could I get?" but "How much do I need to reach a specific milestone, with time to spare?"

1. **Name the milestone.** Pick a result that would make the company self-sustaining or much easier to raise money for next time, such as 50 paying customers, a finished pilot with two hospitals or a regulatory approval.
2. **Estimate how long it will take**, then add time. Most things take longer than founders expect.
3. **Estimate your monthly spending**, often called burn: any salaries or stipends, contractors, software, marketing, legal and accounting.
4. **Add a buffer** for delays and for the months it may take to raise again or reach profitability.

> A fictional team building appointment reminders for small veterinary clinics expects to spend $15,000 a month. They think reaching 50 paying clinics will take 12 months, and they want a six-month buffer. That's 18 months × $15,000 = $270,000.

Raising too little can leave you out of money just short of the milestone. Raising far more than you need can mean giving away more of the company than necessary. Investors at this stage often ask what their money will achieve, and a specific milestone is easier to believe than a big round number.

## How early rounds are structured

This section explains common instruments in general terms. What's available to you depends on your country and how your company is set up, so have a lawyer review any real document before you sign it.

### The SAFE (a US practice)

A SAFE, short for simple agreement for future equity, was introduced by Y Combinator in 2013, and YC publishes standard versions on its [documents page](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents). The standard version is designed for US companies. Some founders elsewhere use it, but many countries have their own alternatives; in the UK, for example, founders often use an advance subscription agreement.

With a SAFE, an investor gives you money now and receives shares later, usually when you raise a priced round. There's no interest and no repayment date. Two terms decide how many shares the investor eventually gets:

- **The valuation cap** sets the highest company value at which the SAFE converts. YC's standard SAFE uses a post-money cap, which counts the money raised on SAFEs but not the new money from the later priced round.
- **The discount**, if there is one, lets the investor convert at a lower price than new investors pay in that round, for example 20% lower.

When a SAFE has both, it generally converts at whichever price gives the investor more shares. If the priced round values the company above the cap, the investor's share just before the new money comes in follows a simple formula: **ownership = investment ÷ post-money cap**. If the round is priced below the cap, they convert at the lower price and end up with more.

> A fictional company making booking software for physiotherapy clinics raises $250,000 on SAFEs with a $2.5 million post-money cap. One angel puts in $50,000, so her share is $50,000 ÷ $2,500,000 = 2%. All the SAFEs together come to $250,000 ÷ $2,500,000 = 10%, so the founders own 90% once the SAFEs convert. A year later, new investors put $1.5 million into a priced round at a $6 million pre-money valuation, so the post-money valuation is $7.5 million and they own $1.5 million ÷ $7.5 million = 20%. Everyone else's share shrinks by a fifth: the angel goes from 2% to 1.6%, and the founders from 90% to 72%.

One consequence is easy to miss: with post-money SAFEs, each extra SAFE you sign reduces the founders' share, not the earlier SAFE investors'. Keep a running total.

### Convertible notes

A convertible note also turns into shares at a later round, often with a cap and a discount, but it's a loan. It carries interest, which usually converts into shares too, and a maturity date. If the note hasn't converted by then, the investor may be able to demand repayment, which can be serious for a company with little cash. Notes are common in many countries, including where SAFEs aren't standard.

### Priced rounds

In a priced round, you sell shares now at an agreed price, which sets the company's valuation. Investors often get extra rights, such as being repaid first if the company is sold, though this varies with local law and tax rules. Priced rounds cost more in legal work, but in some countries they're normal even at the earliest stage.

### Dilution

Each time the company issues new shares, to investors, employees or advisors, every existing owner's percentage shrinks, even though they hold the same number of shares. That's dilution. It isn't bad in itself, since a smaller share of a more valuable company can be worth more, but it adds up over several rounds, and option pools for employees usually dilute founders further. Keep an up-to-date cap table, the record of who owns what, and model how each new agreement changes it before you sign.

## Find investors and run the process

### What early investors look for

At pre-seed there's little data, so investors mostly weigh a few questions. Why is this team the right one, and what do you understand about the problem that others don't? How painful is the problem, and for how many people? What evidence do you have, such as pilots, early revenue or users who keep coming back? What will this money change? Clear, honest answers matter more than polished slides.

### Build a list and track it

Early investors include angels (individuals investing their own money), pre-seed funds, angel networks, accelerators that invest in exchange for equity, and in some countries government-backed funds or regulated crowdfunding platforms. Many countries limit who may invest in a private company and how you may advertise a raise, which is another reason to speak to a lawyer first.

Build a list of several dozen names in a spreadsheet, and track for each one:

- how much they typically invest
- whether they back companies at your stage, in your sector and in your country
- how you're connected, and who could introduce you
- status: not contacted, intro requested, met, following up, committed or passed
- the next step and its date

Introductions from someone who knows you both tend to get more replies, but a short, specific cold email with real evidence can work too. Try to hold first meetings within a few weeks of each other, so conversations move in parallel.

### The meeting and the follow-up

A first meeting often lasts 20 to 30 minutes. Explain what you do in a sentence or two, show the product if you can and know your numbers. Answer directly; "I don't know, but I'll find out and email you" is a fine answer. Ask your own questions too: how they decide, how long it takes and what they'd need to see.

Within a day, send a short thank-you with anything you promised, and ask whether they'd like your monthly update. If they pass, thank them and ask what would change their mind; a "no" now can become a "yes" later. Enthusiasm in a meeting isn't a commitment. Until documents are signed and the money has arrived, keep talking to other investors.

## Red flags in terms

Know the warning signs, and slow down and talk to a lawyer if you see any of these:

- **Fees to be considered.** Most investors never charge founders to look at their company, and some fee schemes are scams.
- **Pressure to sign immediately**, or being discouraged from having a lawyer review the documents.
- **A very large share for a small amount of money**, which can leave too little ownership for you and for later investors.
- **Control at the earliest stage**, such as a majority of the board or a veto over ordinary business decisions.
- **Unusual payout terms**, such as receiving several times their money back before anyone else if the company is sold.
- **Finders who want a cut** of the money raised in return for introductions. This is regulated in many countries.
- **Promises that aren't in the documents.** If it matters, get it in writing.

Before accepting money, ask to speak with founders the investor has backed, including some whose companies struggled. How investors behave when things go badly tells you a lot.

## Do the work

Open the raise plan builder and start with the hardest field: why raise now, or why not. Deciding not to raise is a valid result; if that's yours, fill in the rest as a plan for later and focus on what you'll do instead.

1. Write the milestone first, then work out the amount and the runway it buys from your monthly spending, as in the veterinary example.
2. Name the instrument you expect to use, noting that a lawyer will review it.
3. Build your investor list by type, such as angels, funds, accelerators and grant programs. Start with at least five names and keep adding.
4. Write your pitch in three sentences: what you do and for whom, your best evidence, and what this money will achieve.
5. List the materials you have ready, such as a short deck, a one-page summary, a demo and your cap table.
6. Set a timeline with a decision date, and write what you'll do if you haven't raised by then.

You're done when someone who doesn't know your company could read the plan and understand why you are or aren't raising, how much, what it achieves, who you'll ask and what happens if it doesn't work out.
